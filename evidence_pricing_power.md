# Evidence: Governments, not Via, set the price

Tags follow THESIS_BRIEF.md: **[F]** filing · **[P]** public records / press · **[M]** our estimate · **[V]** verify before using

## 0. Correct the RideCo framing before using it

- **LA Metro no longer uses RideCo.**
  - RideCo held Contract PS46292001 (2020 to Mar 2025): software plus operations management, with Metro's own employees driving **[P]**
  - Since **Mar 16, 2025**, Via runs the vehicles (Crosby Street Transit LLC, formerly Nomad Transit LLC, "a wholly owned subsidiary of Via Transportation") and Spare supplies the software **[P]**
- **We found no cuts to RideCo's price.** Every RideCo modification we found raised the contract value: $29.2M → $35.1M (2020) → $43.4M (Apr 2023) → $57.3M (Mod 11, Oct 2023) **[P]**
- **The fall in cost per hour came from Metro's own changes.** Metro cut cost per revenue hour from **$147 to $101** (June 2023 → Q2 FY24) by reducing driver overtime, raising the fare to $2.50 and adjusting zones. Via was not in the program then, so a chart showing "Via fleet costs constant" alongside RideCo cuts needs its source checked **[P]**
- **The patent royalty is confirmed, with limits.**
  - Via's release (Sep 17, 2025): a "10% running royalty on all future U.S. sales of RideCo, Inc.'s **infringing products**", not on all RideCo sales **[F]**
  - No injunction was entered **[F]**
  - RideCo has deposited ~$4.7M in escrow pending appeal **[V]**

**The LA record makes a stronger point than the RideCo framing.** Section 1 walks through it.

## 1. LA Metro Micro: the agency set the price of both the software and the operations

Sources: LA Metro board reports 2024-0989 (Nov 21, 2024), 2024-1148 (Jan 16, 2025), 2025-0473 (Jul 17, 2025) **[P]**

**a) The agency's board set the target price.** In Motion 42 (Sep 2023), the board directed staff to reach **$20–25 per trip**, against an actual $42. The new procurement was designed around that number: "the goal of a more cost competitive solution."

**b) The software was bid down to zero.**
| Hourly rate (per revenue service hour) | Old model | New model |
|---|---|---|
| Software | $3.67 (RideCo) | **$0.00** (Spare: $0.01 for 3 yrs, $0.00 option) |
| Operations | $98.13 (Metro in-house) | **$82.35** (Via) |
| Total | $101.80 | $82.35 |
| Cost per trip at 3.4 riders/hr | $29.94 | $24.22 |

- Spare bid ~$0 because it "sees benefit in partnering with Metro to strengthen their market presence" after raising a $42M Series B.
- Via's own 10-K names the practice: competitors "submit bids to provide their services for a nominal amount … it could … reduce the market price for our services." **[F]**

**c) Via won operations only by underbidding everyone.**
- Via bid **$82.35/hr**. The six other proposals came in at **$101.44–$147.88/hr**, so Via was **~19% below the next-lowest bidder** (82.35 vs 101.44) and 16% below Metro's own cost.
- That $82.35 must cover drivers, vehicles, insurance, maintenance, fuel, a storage facility, security and on-site management.
- **Metro also set Via's labor cost:**
  - Metro's Living Wage policy applies. Via committed to **$28.28/hr for drivers** and $28.00 for mechanics, with escalators
  - The driver wage alone is 34% of the rate before benefits and payroll taxes **[M]**
- **Via carries the volume risk:**
  - The contract is Indefinite Delivery/Indefinite Quantity (IDIQ) with a not-to-exceed ceiling, so Metro orders hours as needed
  - North: $45.0M (3-year base) + $47.1M (option); South: $21.0M + $22.0M
- The 10-K admits this kind of bid: "we may price our initial bid … at a level that we do not expect to meet our profitability goals in the near term." **[F]**

**d) The largest agency split the bundle.** Metro separated software from operations "to allow for operational control and financial sustainability." At one of Via's largest US deployments, Via now earns **only services revenue at an operator's price and no software revenue**. This supports Thesis 1 (bus contractor) and the take-rate argument.

## 2. Via's own filings describe a buyer's market (FY25 10-K) **[F]**

- "We are also likely to face increasing purchasing scrutiny during the renewal process with our large customers, as these contracts often have to be rebid in competitive public procurements, which may result in **decreased contract size or scope or increased pricing pressure**."
- Risk of "winning by **bidding so low that our margins are impacted**."
- "If we overestimate these costs, we may not be awarded the contract as the **lowest priced responsive bidder**."
- Contracts "frequently contain … **termination for convenience** provisions, discretion for the government customer to **suspend or delay performance**, and government options for future periods."
- Government customers are **>90% of revenue** (FY24 and FY25).

## 4. Why governments hold the pricing power

1. **Price is set by a target cost per trip**, and the agency controls the other input, riders per hour, through zones, fares and hours (LA Motion 42). Via can't raise price without breaking the program's economics.
2. **The software is competed to zero:** Spare's $0.01 at LA, and Spare named in the 10-K as "nominal amount" bidders. The high-margin layer is the easiest part to take out.
3. **Agencies set labor and procurement terms:**
   - Wage floors (LA Living Wage)
   - Union carry-over (Rochester, Mobile)
   - Disadvantaged-business subcontracting goals (LA: Via committed 0.73% against a 20% goal and passed only on appeal)
4. **Volume is the customer's option:** IDIQ contracts, not-to-exceed ceilings, termination for convenience, and hour cuts mid-contract (Jersey City).
5. **Renewals are rebids:** each re-procurement resets price against Spare, RideCo, Transdev, MV and the in-house option.

**What this means for the model:**
- Hold $/hr flat or falling in real terms at renewal
- Model software fees as at risk of being unbundled at large agencies
- Don't assume price escalators cover driver wage growth
